## Why this decision has real consequences

Childcare software is compliance infrastructure, not a productivity tool. The attendance records it generates are what your state licensing officer reviews during an inspection. The billing data it exports is what your subsidy agency uses to verify reimbursements. If the software fails an audit, wrong format, missing timestamps, ratio gaps, that is a licensing violation, not a wasted afternoon.

Most centers try two tools before finding one that holds up. Each switch costs staff training time, data migration effort, and a gap in attendance records during the transition.

## Step 1: List your daily admin tasks

Before opening any vendor website, write down what staff do every day.

For most licensed centers: log each child's arrival and departure, verify staff-to-child ratios by room, document any medication or incidents, send a daily update to parents, and pull attendance records at month-end for subsidy billing.

That is your baseline. Software that does those five things in a format your state agency accepts is more valuable than software with 40 features you will never use.

If a demo spends most of its time on photo sharing and learning documentation while rushing through attendance and compliance, that tells you where the vendor's development resources go.

## Step 2: Check subsidy and compliance features for your state

Subsidy billing is state-specific, not generic.

Texas centers billing through the Texas Workforce Commission need attendance records that match Local Workforce Development Board requirements. California centers working with Alternative Payment agencies face a patchwork of regional agencies with different reporting requirements. Florida centers in the School Readiness program bill through one of 31 Early Learning Coalitions.

Ask the vendor: "Can you show me the report I would submit to [your specific state agency] for monthly subsidy billing?" A capable vendor pulls that up in the demo. If they show you a generic attendance export and say to "work with your agency on format," that means you will do manual formatting every month.

## Step 3: Compare total cost including per-child fees

The advertised price is rarely the number that matters.

Per-child pricing scales with enrollment. At 50 children, a $2/child tool costs $100/month. At 150 children it costs $300. A flat-base plan at {{plan.center_starter.priceLabel}} can be cheaper before a center reaches full licensed capacity.

Calculate your bill at three enrollment levels: current, 20% growth, and full licensed capacity.

Also check: setup fees, training fees, additional user seats, and data export fees. Some vendors charge for exports or make migration deliberately difficult.

## Step 4: Test ratio tracking in real classroom conditions

Ratio tracking only works if staff use it. Staff only use it if it is fast enough not to interfere with childcare.

During pickup at a busy center, 10-15 families arrive in 15 minutes. Staff are greeting parents, helping children collect belongings, and managing transitions while maintaining documented ratios.

Test the software during pickup. Count how many taps it takes to log a departure. Watch whether staff can do it without stopping a conversation. Watch what happens when two families arrive together.

A tool that needs a dedicated staff member at a tablet to log departures is an additional staff cost, not a compliance solution.

## Step 5: Confirm data export and migration support

Your attendance records are legal documents. State licensing audits look back two to three years.

Before signing up, ask: Can I export all attendance records, billing history, and family contacts? In what format? If I cancel, how long do I have access to my data?

Vendors who provide clean CSV or PDF exports of complete historical records protect your ability to switch. Vendors who export only summary data, or who require a support ticket and waiting period for exports, are building switching costs in deliberately.

Your licensing history does not belong to your software vendor.
